**Principles confirmed by 3+ sources:**

1. **Global GSAP plugin registration is mandatory**: `ScrollTrigger`, `SplitText`, `useGSAP`, `Flip` etc. must be registered via `gsap.registerPlugin(...)` at app startup, or animations silently fail/error at runtime.
2. **Scroll-bound scrubbing**: `scrub: true` or a numeric smoothing value (0.5/1/1.5/2) ties timeline progress directly to scroll position for real-time forward/reverse playback.
3. **Viewport pinning**: `pin: true` in ScrollTrigger locks a section/canvas in the viewport for a scroll distance (`start`/`end` like `"top top"`→`"+=2000"`), letting foreground content animate while page scroll is suspended.
4. **React lifecycle scoping**: `useGSAP()` (from `@gsap/react`) or `gsap.context()` scoped to a `useRef` container is required for clean teardown of tweens/ScrollTriggers on unmount — avoids React 18 strict-mode double-fire bugs.
5. **3D scene graph traversal**: loading `.glb`/`.gltf` requires `gltf.scene.traverse((child)=>{...})` to set `castShadow`/`receiveShadow`/material tweaks per-mesh.
6. **Smooth-scroll + ScrollTrigger bridging**: custom smooth-scroll libs (Lenis, Locomotive Scroll) need `ScrollTrigger.scrollerProxy()` or manual update listeners (`locoScroll.on("scroll", ScrollTrigger.update)`) to keep viewport math in sync.
7. **Clip-path reveals**: animating CSS `clip-path` polygons (clipped → full rect) inside GSAP timelines is the dominant technique for dramatic image/video/section reveals across award-winning sites.

### General web-design fundamentals & 2026 standards

**Sources extracted 2026-08-31 via direct WebFetch (short blog/article sources — skips the NotebookLM synthesis step per this file's own sourcing-pipeline rule, since these are already short enough that a synthesis pass would only lose detail, not add it).**

**Nielsen Norman Group — "Design Priorities" (Jakob Nielsen):**
- Top 3 design priorities, in order: (1) communicate clearly so users understand you, (2) provide the information users actually want, (3) offer simple, consistent page design with clear navigation.
- Core principle: **"You are not the user" — design to optimize the experience for outsiders, not insiders** (people who already know the business/product).
- Real cited failures: an e-commerce site lost millions because overly aggressive homepage promotions made users distrust the pricing; a service company lost customers because its store finder required knowing the company's own name per location; a content site drove users away with intrusive advertising.
- **"3D is nearly useless in most cases"** — eyetracking studies show glamour illustrations get zero fixations while users hunt for actual product info; product images that are too small/fuzzy/badly-angled are a real, common failure.
- Core recommendation: **focus on the basics customers value, not on "what's hot."** (Directly relevant tension with the "2026 trends" sources below — see the synthesis note at the end of this section.)

**Nielsen Norman Group — "10 High-Profit Redesign Priorities" (Jakob Nielsen):**
- Core principle: "Interface design is about making money for the company."
- The 10 ranked priorities: (1) **email newsletters — "probably the single-highest ROI action you can take"**; (2) informative product pages (poor product info caused 8% of usability problems, 10% of user failures in tested sites); (3) high-quality photography linked to near-full-screen enlargements; (4) product differentiation/comparison tools (addresses "fear of buying the wrong product"); (5) support for reordering repeat purchases; (6) simplified text — **"you can usually double website or intranet usability simply by rewriting the text"**; (7) catering to seniors (the fastest-growing internet-user segment); (8) gift-giving support (wishlists/gift certificates); (9) search — needs meaningful page titles and copy written in the user's own vocabulary, not just a search box; (10) user testing — near-zero cost via cheap paper prototypes. Bonus: loyalty programs modeled on airline frequent-flyer miles.

**Nielsen Norman Group — "Trustworthy Design":**
- **4 core trustworthiness factors** (originally Nielsen 1999, reconfirmed 2016): Design Quality (professional appearance/organization — meaningful nav labels, appropriate color scheme, zero typos/broken links, adequate white space); Upfront Disclosure (contact info in utility nav, base costs+fees shown before requesting billing info, prominent return policies, no lengthy gated quote forms); Comprehensive/Correct/Current Content (photos from ALL service stages not just end results, full service range shown, testimonials); **Connected to the Rest of the Web** (external reviews, social presence, FAQ pages).
- **"Users trust third-party review sites MORE than reviews listed on the website itself."** Login walls / gated content breach trust specifically by "asking for information before providing any value" — a real, quotable justification for never gating a portfolio or pricing behind a signup form.

**Nielsen Norman Group — "Scrolling and Attention":**
- Real eyetracking numbers: users spend ~57% of page-viewing time above the fold; 74% of viewing time in the first two screenfuls; 81% in the first three screenfuls. On search-results-style pages specifically, 47% of time is on the top 20% of the page, 75%+ in the top 40%.
- **The F-pattern**: users look most thoroughly at text near the top, with attention dropping fixation-by-fixation further down the page.
- **"Illusion of completeness"**: a real named failure mode where a minimalist design makes a page LOOK finished at the fold, so users don't realize there's more below and never scroll — counter with visible "signifiers" (e.g. deliberately cut-off text/images at the fold edge) that hint more content follows.
- Practical rules: reserve the very top of the page for the highest-priority content (key business+user goals); keep major CTAs above the fold; use headers/bold text to flag important info at every scroll depth; **"beware of false floors" in minimalist designs specifically**. Stated finding: **"people will scroll if they have a reason to" — modern larger screens haven't meaningfully reduced how much users scroll**, so a long page is not inherently a problem if the content earns the scroll.

- **Synthesis note, resolving an apparent tension with the NN/G sources above**: NN/G's "focus on basics, not what's hot" (written pre-2016, before today's AI/3D/motion toolset existed) is not actually in conflict with adopting 2026 visual trends — it's a warning against decoration that HURTS usability (illegible product photos, useless 3D, gated content), not against modern craft itself.
